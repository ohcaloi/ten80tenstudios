The 10/80/10 rule: how much of your week can actually be delegated?

Delegation fails when you hand someone a job title instead of a system. 10/80/10 hands them a running machine.

Ask a founder how much of their week could be delegated and most say twenty percent, maybe thirty. When we map it task by task, the honest number is closer to eighty. The gap isn't ability. It's method.

Why delegation usually fails

Here's the standard playbook. You hire an assistant. You spend three weeks explaining your tools, your clients, your quirks. Things improve for a while — until a process changes, or the assistant moves on, and every hour you invested walks out the door with them.

The real problem: you didn't delegate the work. You took on a second job — workflow designer, trainer and quality controller — and called it delegation. No wonder it feels harder than doing the task yourself.

The rule, spelled out

10% — Setup. Once, at the start. We sit with you, map how the work actually flows, and build the system around it — follow-ups, intake, reminders, reporting. You make the design decisions here, and only here.

80% — Delegated. The middle of every process is repetitive: enter, send, chase, update, file. Automation does the purely mechanical part, and a trained specialist runs the rest on top of the system. You talk to one person. They own the outcome.

10% — Review. Your remaining job: look at results, approve, and flag anything that needs judgment. Ten minutes a day instead of four hours.

What belongs in the 80

If a task repeats weekly and follows rules you could write on one page, it belongs in the 80. In most service businesses that covers:

  • Lead follow-up and appointment scheduling
  • Data entry, CRM hygiene and weekly reporting
  • Invoicing, payment reminders and gentle chasing
  • Inbox triage and routine client updates
  • Intake forms, onboarding steps and status checks

What stays in your two 10s

The first 10 is leverage: deciding how work should flow, once, instead of re-deciding it every day. The last 10 is judgment: approving output, catching exceptions, setting direction. Both are genuinely your job. Everything between them is not.

Notice what's missing: managing. You never have to direct anyone, because the workflow was designed for you — that's the whole point of the first 10%.

Why the split works when job titles don't

A job description says "handle admin." A system says: when a lead arrives, send this reply within five minutes; if no response in two days, send that one; log everything here; escalate anything unusual to the owner. One is a hope. The other is a machine with a person at the wheel.

That's also why the 80 can be delegated safely to someone you've never trained yourself. Your specialist isn't guessing at your preferences — they're operating a workflow that already encodes them. The system carries the memory, so quality doesn't depend on any one person's recall, including yours.

The numbers behind the rule

Across our clients, up to 80% of repetitive workflows end up automated or delegated, and teams reclaim about 35 hours of manual work per month. The first automation is usually live in about two weeks — small enough to trust, fast enough to feel.

Find your own 80

Try this before you hire anyone or buy anything. For three working days, keep a running list of every task you touch. Mark the ones that repeat and follow rules. Most people stare at that list and see the same thing we do: a business where the owner is the busiest employee — and doesn't need to be.

That list is your automation map. We'll happily build the rest of it with you.
